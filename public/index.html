Brighter mornings or longer evenings? See whether permanent daylight saving time or permanent standard time fits your life better.

You’re choosing where the hour goes.

Permanent standard time keeps the winter clock. Permanent daylight saving time keeps the summer clock. The choice is whether the same hour of daylight falls earlier in your morning or later in your evening.

03/The tradeoffs

There’s a good reason for each.

Your lifestyle preference and the public-health evidence can point in different directions. Both are useful considerations.

The case for standard time.

Protect the morning. Let evening arrive earlier.

+

Earlier light for early starts

Sunrise arrives an hour earlier than under daylight saving. Depending on your location and schedule, that can put sunlight into the school run, commute, or morning walk. This is a direct consequence of the clock shift.

+

The stronger case from sleep science

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends permanent standard time because it better aligns with circadian biology. Morning light helps set the body clock; later evening light can shift it later. [1]

−

Earlier sunsets, all year

Compared with daylight saving, you lose an hour at the evening end. In winter, the sun may already be down when work ends. Summer sunsets also arrive an hour earlier than the familiar summer clock.

−

Some summer light arrives while you sleep

In northern or eastern parts of a time zone, sunrise can be very early. If you sleep through that extra morning hour, it may feel less useful than light after work. Check your own windows above.

The case for daylight saving.

Keep the evening. Accept a later morning.

+

Later light after work

Sunset arrives an hour later on the clock. With a fixed workday, that can mean more sunlit time for exercise, play, errands, or dinner outside. The gain depends on when your free time starts and ends.

+

Keep the familiar summer evenings

Where seasonal daylight saving is observed, permanent daylight saving preserves summer sunrise and sunset clock times. It also avoids the autumn clock change that makes sunset suddenly appear an hour earlier.

−

Winter mornings get darker

The one-hour delay applies in December and January, too. Sunrise can move beyond the start of work or school, especially far north or near the western edge of a time zone. The city selector makes that difference visible.

−

Later light has a biological cost

More evening light and less morning light can push sleep timing later, while work and school still start at the same clock time. Sleep organizations favor standard time; individual schedules and actual light exposure vary. [2]

Both options end “spring forward” and “fall back”.

Either permanent option removes the recurring spring and autumn switches. You would keep the same clock all year, without losing or gaining an hour twice a year. [2]

Energy savings are a separate, uncertain argument. A US Department of Energy study found a small electricity saving from extending seasonal daylight saving by four weeks in 2007. That result is not a measurement of permanent daylight saving. [3]

How the comparison works ↓

A brief history of time zones and daylight saving
  1. 1883

    Railroads adopt time zones

    U.S. and Canadian railroads coordinate four time zones, replacing a patchwork of local solar times.

  2. 1918

    The first federal daylight saving law

    The Standard Time Act establishes federal time zones and seasonal daylight saving during World War I.

  3. 1919

    Daylight saving is repealed

    Congress overrides President Wilson’s veto. Federal daylight saving ends; some communities keep it locally.

  4. 1942–1945

    “War Time” brings it back

    Year-round daylight saving returns during World War II, ending in September 1945. Local differences then return.

  5. 1966

    A uniform seasonal schedule

    The Uniform Time Act sets nationwide change dates while allowing states to stay on standard time.

  6. 1974–1975

    A year-round experiment

    An energy-crisis trial starts in January 1974. Congress restores standard time that October for four months; seasonal switching resumes in 1975.

  7. 1987

    An earlier spring change

    A 1986 law moves the start of daylight saving to the first Sunday in April.

  8. 2007

    The season gets longer

    A 2005 law takes effect: daylight saving runs from the second Sunday in March to the first Sunday in November.

  9. 2022

    The Senate backs permanent daylight saving

    The Sunshine Protection Act passes the Senate but does not become law.

  10. July 2026

    The debate continues

    The House passes another Sunshine Protection Act proposal. Congress also considers proposals for permanent standard time.

Timeline sources: U.S. Department of Transportation and Congressional Research Service (July 2026 report).

The assumptions behind the sunlight

All dates are in 2026. Sunrise, sunset, and civil twilight are calculated in your browser with SunCalc 1.9.0 using each location’s latitude and longitude and a level, unobstructed horizon. Times are approximate and rounded to the nearest minute. Terrain, altitude, clouds, and atmospheric conditions affect the light you experience. The landscape is an illustration: its sky colors blend gradually around dawn and dusk, while the labels follow the exact sunrise, sunset, and civil-twilight cutoffs. The colors do not represent weather.

“Standard” uses the selected location’s fixed standard UTC offset. “Daylight saving” uses that offset plus one hour on every date. Both are hypothetical permanent clocks.

The optional seasonal line models the US March–November rule: daylight saving from the second Sunday in March through the day before the first Sunday in November. It is a comparison schedule, not a prediction about legislation. Arizona and Hawaii cities in this list omit seasonal changes. The 2026 change dates are March 8 and November 1.

“Without sunlight at departure” counts days when the sun is below the horizon at departure, including twilight. “Sunlight left at free-time start” counts days when the sun is up at that time. These are daylight opportunities; they do not measure your actual outdoor exposure.

How your preference is calculated

For every day, we calculate the minutes between sunrise and sunset that overlap your morning window and your evening window. We add those minutes across all 365 days. Your routine stays at the same clock times under both options, including weekends. Twilight is excluded.

The switch picks the window that matters more to you. We compare the annual sunlit hours in that window under each clock, and show what the winning clock does to your other window. The result only describes this sunlight preference; it does not combine sleep outcomes, economics, safety, weather, or personal health. Your selected date affects the day preview, while this comparison always uses the full year.

Your settings are saved in this browser when local storage is available. “Share this view” creates a link containing the location, date, routine, and preferences. The explorer uses no location tracking or analytics.
